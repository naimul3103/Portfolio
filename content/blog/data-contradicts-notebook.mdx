The first week of an applied-ML internship handed me a notebook and a dataset:
30,000 anonymised content rows, with impressions, position, click-through rate
and a declining label. The notebook walked through a first look and stated a
familiar story — CTR falls as average position gets worse.

I ran the numbers. The story did not survive.

## What the data said

Three checks, each one a single cell:

| Question | Result | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Does search volume explain impressions? | r = 0.001 | No relationship at all |
| Does position explain CTR? | r = −0.073 | Almost independent |
| Where is the worst CTR? | 0.000, top-3 comparison articles | Not where the narrative points |

The first result needed a filter to be meaningful: rows with zero impressions
make the correlation look like something it is not. Restricting to
`impressions_90d > 0` changed the coefficient from 0.001 to 0.001 — the rare case
where the filter confirms the null result instead of rescuing it.

## Why this was uncomfortable

The notebook was written by people more experienced than me, and it came with a
narrative that made sense. The temptation is to find the cut of the data that
reproduces the expected answer. Position tiers, content types, a subset of
traffic — somewhere there is always a slice that agrees.

The discipline is to decide the question before looking, and then to report the
answer even when it is boring. "These two variables are unrelated in this
dataset" is a finding. It is just not a headline.

## What I would do differently

I edited the supplied notebook instead of working in my own copy, so my analysis
is buried in a template and a reviewer has to dig for it. Keep your own work in
your own files, even when the exercise invites you to edit theirs.

The second thing: I would write the research question first. I did the analysis
first and reverse-engineered the question afterwards, which is how you end up
with three interesting results and no argument connecting them.
